# Makepad Scope shares glyph3d's premise, not its engine

The short answer is that someone has built the idea and no one we found has built the machine. Showing a whole repository as a zoomable field of real, readable source text already exists. **Makepad Scope** (Rik Arends, demoed from 2026-09-08, closed private beta) flies through Chromium, which he puts at about 51 million lines, at 120 Hz in about 3 GB of RAM. It drops text into a chain of coloured-block LODs that includes a "lines are blocks" tier, close to glyph3d's wash. Back in 2013, **Wideboard** drew the entire Linux kernel as literal text from a GPU-resident byte texture plus a per-line pointer texture. So "first to render a whole codebase as readable text" is not available to claim. Scope's engine, though, is the opposite of glyph3d's Visible mode. It **lays the treemap out once at index time** and streams pre-baked, LZ4-compressed per-LOD geometry off disk, and Rik says outright that it "can't" re-lay the repo out in realtime. glyph3d keeps the raw bytes, line table and glyph trie resident on the GPU and **lays out only the visible lines every frame in compute**: UTF-8 decode, emoji-sequence resolution, wrap and pagination, held byte-for-byte to the CPU engine on both Metal and Vulkan. We found no precedent for that combination, none for wrap-into-depth, none for emoji-sequence resolution inside a GPU layout kernel, and none for a CI battery that mutates its own gates. A write-up is worth doing. It should be an engineering and methods piece that names Scope generously as the contemporary answer to the same premise. It should not be a "first 3D codebase viewer" announcement, and it should never put glyph3d's ~170 ms next to Scope's 60 s as if the two measured the same thing.

## Scope already flies through Chromium, by precomputing everything

Scope is the central comparison. Nearly everything known about its internals comes from Rik's own X replies, because no code, design doc or talk is public. The product is "makepad scope, in private beta atm" ([X](https://x.com/rikarends/status/2100323422135820638)). It is meant to be sold "for a small fee", released "with-source", and open-sourced "later this year likely" ([X](https://x.com/rikarends/status/2099233145904136703)). No branch of the public `makepad/makepad` repo contains it ([GitHub](https://github.com/makepad/makepad)). He describes the layout as a treemap sized "on token count not filesize" ([X](https://x.com/rikarends/status/2097306939373850982)). One screenshot shows an optional 3D mode that extrudes blocks by log(tokens) ([X](https://x.com/rikarends/status/2097393306762191147)). The LOD chain, in his words: "text->colors of the blocks in the text->lines are blocks->areas are blocks->file is a block" ([X](https://x.com/rikarends/status/2099174617009057841)). The decisive architectural statement: "As long as you dont relayout the whole thing in realtime (it can't do that) you can visualise quite nicely" ([X](https://x.com/rikarends/status/2098765277823373597)). Scope "indexes on load / when changed and keeps it on disk sliced per LOD level for continuous reads" ([X](https://x.com/rikarends/status/2099752816705204376)). Its renderer went from a realtime texture-tile cache to "gpu geometry zoom not tile based" ([X](https://x.com/rikarends/status/2100096105107116167)).

One finding corrects a note in this research set, and the Makepad entry on your page. Scope's text is probably not SDF/MSDF. Asked about it, Rik said: "I have long replaced the font rendering with AI generated vector stacks. Currently i think its a bit like the Slug algorithm" ([X](https://x.com/rikarends/status/2100838964672286884)). Makepad's public text stack has carried a Slug atlas since PR #1042, merged 2026-04-16 ([GitHub](https://github.com/makepad/makepad/pull/1042)). Even so, `slug_atlas.rs` says "Slug glyphs are only used for very large text" ([GitHub](https://github.com/makepad/makepad/blob/dev/draw/src/text/slug_atlas.rs)). So whether Scope uses analytic glyphs everywhere, or only near the camera, is unknown. One more caveat applies to all of this. Asked about his index architecture, Rik replied "I honestly have no idea. I just told it to make it fast" ([X](https://x.com/rikarends/status/2097590004285411667)). The code was largely written by AI agents, so even his own descriptions are second-hand.

**The load times cannot be compared.** Scope's 60 s to 2m25 is a full index build: parsing, cross-referencing 15 languages into a graph of roughly 1.3 million edges, and baking LOD geometry. Rik says his CPU cores "weren't saturated" ([X](https://x.com/rikarends/status/2100301088351998432)). glyph3d's 168 ms is glyph layout with no semantic analysis, on a corpus about a twentieth the size. The nearest like-for-like figure is Scope's early "built the map in another 46 [s]", and that is still a precompute. The fair frame is architectural. Scope spends index time and disk to make per-frame work trivial. Its 120 Hz is a density budget it tunes ("It's trivial to overload it", [X](https://x.com/rikarends/status/2099176800366506009)). glyph3d spends per-frame GPU work to make preprocessing almost nothing. **Scope is ahead on corpus size, semantics and product polish. glyph3d is ahead on zero-preprocessing load, a layout that can change every frame, true 3D placement, and provable exactness.** It is fair to say both are answers to the same question. "Glyph3d is faster" does not hold up.

Priority comes out neutral. glyph3d-rs's history starts 2026-08-31 (git log), eight days before Scope's first post. Your own lineage goes back further: an HN commenter linked your Swift predecessor LookAtThat in the 2023 Wideboard thread ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=34758336)). But Visible mode landed 2026-10-10, a month after Scope's demos. The honest framing is two independent lineages arriving at the same premise. Neither derives from the other.

## Wideboard put a whole kernel on the GPU a decade earlier

Scope is the closest contemporary. **Wideboard** (Austin Appleby, 2013, revived 2023) is the closest ancestor of Visible mode's data structure, and your page does not list it. It renders "all of Linux's source code in a browser tab via WebGL" from three textures: one 4096² texture of raw ASCII, one texel per line holding a 24-bit pointer and an 8-bit length, and a Terminus bitmap atlas. That covers about 56,000 files, 31 M lines and 1.2 GB of text at 60 fps ([GitHub](https://github.com/aappleby/Wideboard); [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=34758336)). Resident source bytes plus a resident line table, drawn by a shader, is therefore 13 years old. It ran at roughly ten times glyph3d's flagship byte volume. What Wideboard does not do is layout. It is fixed-width ASCII in 2D, so position is column × cell width. It has no variable advances, no sequences, no wrap, no pagination, no syntax colour and no picking. The same is true of **Zutty**, which renders a terminal grid from a GPU-resident cell array with one compute invocation per cell ([tomscii.sig7.se](https://tomscii.sig7.se/2020/11/How-Zutty-works)). Both show that resident characters drawn by the GPU is an old idea. The new part is the per-frame layout itself.

The other projects sharing the premise are smaller or older. **codescape** is public, built on Makepad and "inspired by Rik Arends' Makepad source visualizer" ([GitHub](https://github.com/WIZARDspace/codescape)). It draws files as flat tiles of real text on a 3D directory relief. Far away it uses one quad per file sampling a mipmapped minimap, where one texel covers two characters of one line. Up close it uses SDF glyph quads capped at 450k. Scan and layout take about 0.6 s for a 1 M-line repo. That far/near split is the nearest public match to glyph3d's wash/glyph tiers, though it is texture-per-file rather than geometry-per-line. One researcher also cited a `flop-labs/codescape` origin; another could not find that org, and the WIZARDspace repo was created 2026-10-10. **code-map** is another Scope-inspired clone, with file boxes and code shown only when zoomed in ([GitHub](https://github.com/abyss464/code-map)). Padioleau's **Codemap** (pfff) and **Code Park** are already on your page. Code Park remains the closest *peer-reviewed* 3D precedent, but it is class-scale ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.02174)). Genome browsers deserve a footnote. igv.js and JBrowse 2 zoom from about 3×10⁹ characters down to readable letters by fetching only the window in view, with hard per-pixel thresholds (letters at 10–12 px per base). But they draw roughly 10²–10³ letters per row in 1D with Canvas2D ([igv.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/igvteam/igv.js/master/js/sequenceTrack.js); [JBrowse](https://github.com/GMOD/jbrowse-components/blob/main/plugins/sequence/src/LinearReferenceSequenceDisplay/components/sequenceGeometry.ts)).

## Every building block has a precedent; the assembly does not

Broken into parts, glyph3d is mostly precedented building blocks. Its novelty sits in a few mechanisms and in the exactness discipline wrapped around them. "No precedent found" means about a hundred targeted web, HN, arXiv and patent-page queries across six research threads came up empty. It does not mean a systematic literature or patent search.

Read together, the table points to one centre of gravity. GPU text layout, as opposed to GPU text rasterization, appears to be unoccupied. Every production stack found places glyphs on the CPU and draws them on the GPU: Slug's layout is "handled in software" ([Slug paper](https://www.sluglibrary.com/slug.pdf)), Parley breaks lines on the CPU ([Linebender](https://linebender.org/blog/tmil-15/)), and HarfBuzz's 2026 GPU push went to rasterization, not shaping. Your repo's "Nobody ships this" holds against every query run here ([out/GPU-DIRECTION-2026-10-09.md](../out/GPU-DIRECTION-2026-10-09.md)). The reason it is open matters for the write-up. Real layout (BiDi, shaping, fallback) belongs to CPU libraries, and editors never need more than a screen of layout per frame. glyph3d's case is unusual: monospace cells, an atlas whose advances are 0, 1 or 2 cells (asserted over all 0x110000 codepoints), no BiDi, and a zoomed-out visible set that can run to millions of lines. Under exactly those conditions a per-line serial walk fits one GPU thread. That is a real contribution with a stated domain. It is not general GPU text layout.

Two smaller points belong in any honest account. The wash tier is old greeking, rebuilt as a per-frame, GPU-culled box sized to the line's back-wrapped depth. Present it as an implementation detail, not an idea. The z-order problem back-wrap creates may be under-documented territory, though. That is a blended glyph pass that has to write depth, at a measured cost of about 1,400 of 1.6 M pixels losing ink at coplanar overlaps in `repo-down` ([AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md)), and no source discussed it. Second, Max Crofts measured a band-less Lengyel shader at 105 ms for just 15,914 glyphs under 16× MSAA ([maxcrofts.com](https://maxcrofts.com/gpu-text-rendering/)). glyph3d's own spike drew 2.5 M sub-pixel Slug glyphs in about 14 ms against 10 ms for flat quads ([out/GPU-DIRECTION-2026-10-09.md](../out/GPU-DIRECTION-2026-10-09.md)). That gap is evidence that LOD tiering is what makes analytic coverage viable at corpus scale. Lengyel's new MIT reference adds MVP-aware "dynamic dilation" for perspective ([Terathon](https://terathon.com/blog/decade-slug.html)), which is worth checking against your shader before the write-up.

## A write-up should sell the method, not the spectacle

The defensible story is not "I rendered a repo in 3D". The repo itself argues the more interesting one: **the line, not the byte, is the parallel unit of text layout**, and holding that design to an oracle produces something new. The arc has three steps. First, the monoid scan was correct and ran "at memory speed" but was "shaped for the wrong problem": it materialized 90 M slots (1.8 GB) when only a few hundred thousand are drawn ([out/GPU-DIRECTION-2026-10-09.md](../out/GPU-DIRECTION-2026-10-09.md)). That negative result is rare in public writing, and it is valuable. Second, Visible mode replaced a ~460 ms, 1.8 GB slot stream with an 11–22 ms backend, about 1.5 B resident per source byte, and ~0.7 ms offscreen frames on a 93 MB tree. Third, its kernel matches the CPU engine slot-for-slot on RTX 5090/Vulkan and M2/Metal (44 of 44 in the prototype), "the property the pixel gates could never claim across vendors" ([out/history/VISIBLE-MODE.md](../out/history/VISIBLE-MODE.md)). Back-wrap is a second, smaller essay: a layout primitive with a visual consequence (z-order) and a measured cost. The verification battery is a third piece, and arguably the most portable. It covers gates that must be shown to redden for a stated reason, coverage reported in place of pass counts, checks that refuse to pass having compared nothing, byte-exact goldens segregated by rasterizer, and golden equivalents across code paths. Graphics engineers outside this project could adopt that method without caring about code visualization at all.

The write-up should claim no precedent found, not "nobody", for per-frame GPU layout from resident bytes with real advances, sequences, wrap and pagination. It should state the domain conditions that make it tractable. It should place glyph3d beside Scope as the per-frame-layout answer to the premise Scope answers by precomputation, credit Wideboard for resident text in 2013, and credit SeeSoft and greeking for the wash. It should not claim to be first to render a codebase as readable or 3D text. It should not claim novelty for Slug-in-WGSL, instancing, greeking or the monoid scan. It should not say it handles the largest corpus, since Scope's Chromium and Wideboard's 1.2 GB are both bigger. It should not claim to be faster than Scope in any form. It should not describe Scope's internals beyond what Rik has posted, and should not repeat the MSDF assumption. It should not claim a comprehension benefit for reading code in 3D. The evidence is mixed: Dominic et al. found higher task load with no productivity difference ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9486787/)). The 168 ms / 577 MB/s figures should be **re-measured before they appear anywhere**, because the repo itself flags them as predating C22's windowed staging and Visible mode.

## These verdicts rest on web-scale search, not a literature review

Coverage limits shape every "no precedent found" above. Google Scholar and Google Patents could not be queried directly. The patent picture comes from keyword-level web search and a handful of individual patent pages. Nobody browsed CPC classes (G06T 11/60, G06F 40/103) or ran assignee searches (Apple, Microsoft, Adobe, NVIDIA, JetBrains), and no claims were read in full. **None of this is a freedom-to-operate or patentability opinion.** The Scope picture comes from X posts read through a mirror plus four video thumbnails. Researchers could not view the videos, could not access the Makepad Discord, and could not see the non-public `rik2` branch. If Scope ships with source as promised, every **I** in the table above becomes checkable, and some may flip. Reddit, YouTube and academic GPU venues (HPG, i3D) were weakly covered. The emoji-on-GPU null result rests on a single query. Scope also drew several clones within two weeks, so a search in a month will probably find more. Finally, glyph3d's own figures come from its repo reports: the Instanced-mode load figures predate later changes and are flagged stale, and the Visible-mode figures come from one Linux box and one M2.

## Conclusion

The research changes what glyph3d is *for*, more than it changes whether it is novel. Before September 2026 the premise looked like the contribution. Your page framed the slot as "real text, whole repo, GPU-instanced" and noted that no prior occupant had survived. Scope has now taken that slot visibly, and it holds it with a precompute architecture that its author says cannot re-lay the field out in realtime. That leaves glyph3d with the counter-position. Its field is laid out live from bytes, so wrap width, mode and per-byte styling are inputs to every frame rather than a reason to re-index, and it is exact enough to prove that across GPU vendors. That position is the stronger claim to write about, because it is a technique other people can use. A visualizer competes with Scope; a published method for oracle-checked, visible-only GPU text layout does not.

Done in this order, the write-up and the prior-art page support each other. First update the page with the Scope, Wideboard and GPU-layout lineages, so the essay can cite its neighbours rather than discover them. Then re-measure, then publish the "line is the parallel unit" piece with its negative result included. Naming Scope generously costs nothing and reads as confidence. The two systems are the clearest available demonstration that the same premise admits two opposite engines.
